LoRA fine-tuning with no GPU and no excuses.

The constraint was fixed: a CPU-only machine and a real deadline. Full fine-tuning was off the table, so the question became how little of the model I actually needed to train.

The constraint was real.

Not a thought experiment about efficiency — an actual machine with no accelerator and a date the work had to be done by. Full fine-tuning of even a 124M-parameter model means holding weights, gradients, and optimizer state simultaneously. That was not going to fit.

LoRA changes the shape of the problem. Instead of updating the base weights, you freeze them entirely and train small low-rank adapter matrices injected alongside the attention projections. The base model contributes its knowledge; the adapters contribute the task.

from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model

config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,                      # rank — the capacity dial
    lora_alpha=32,             # scaling, conventionally 2x rank
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    target_modules=["c_attn"],  # attention projections
    bias="none",
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)

model = get_peft_model(base_model, config)
model.print_trainable_parameters()
# trainable: 589,824 || all: 125,029,632 || trainable%: 0.4717
On rank: 16 was not arbitrary. Rank controls how much the adapter can express — too low and it can't learn the task, too high and you've given back the memory savings. For a narrow task on a clean dataset, 16 was sufficient.

Data quality beat everything else.

The first runs underperformed. The instinct in that moment is to reach for hyperparameters — raise the rank, adjust the learning rate, train longer. On a CPU, every one of those experiments costs hours you don't have.

So I looked at the inputs instead. The training data had formatting inconsistencies that meant the model was partly learning to predict formatting noise rather than the task. Fixing the data format improved results more than any hyperparameter change did — and cost a fraction of the compute to verify.

The transferable lesson

When a fine-tune underperforms, look at the data before you look at the configuration. It's almost always cheaper, and it's usually the answer.

The comparison that matters to a customer

I ran the same task as a GPT-3.5-turbo fine-tune through the OpenAI API. Not because one approach wins, but because the tradeoff is exactly what an enterprise has to decide:

ApproachWhat you're choosing
Self-hosted LoRAControl. Data never leaves your environment, the adapter is yours, and inference cost is your infrastructure cost. You operate it.
Managed APIConvenience. Better base model, no infrastructure, faster to a result. Your data transits a third party and you're on their pricing and deprecation schedule.

For data-sensitive customers in regulated industries, that first row is frequently not a preference — it's the only architecture that gets approved. Having actually run both means I can describe the tradeoff from experience rather than from a comparison table.
